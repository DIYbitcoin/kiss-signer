01 / start here

How signing works

Your keys stay on this device. Everything that needs the internet happens somewhere else. That other app is called a coordinator.

  1. Pair a watch-only wallet

    KISS shows a QR holding your public keys, called a descriptor or a zpub. Your online wallet can then see your balance and give out addresses, but it holds nothing that can spend.

  2. Build the transaction there

    It picks the coins and the fee and makes a PSBT, a transaction with no signature on it yet. Nothing can move.

  3. Verify and sign here

    Pass the PSBT over by QR or microSD. KISS works out every amount for itself, shows you, and signs only after a deliberate hold.

  4. Broadcast from the coordinator

    Take the signed transaction back the same way and your online wallet sends it. KISS never touched the network.

Pairing offers Sparrow on desktop and BlueWallet on mobile. Others work if they do both halves: import a descriptor or a zpub as watch-only, and pass PSBTs back by QR or microSD. Ask in Telegram about anything untried.

02 / what it does

What it does

DECOY SIGNER

It boots into a game

FRUIT ISLAND is a real, playable game. One secret gesture on its menu opens the signer, a different one opens a spare set of keys, so there is always something to show.

TOUCHSCREEN + QR

Transactions in and out by QR, or microSD

A whole transaction will not fit in one QR code. So KISS chops it into numbered pieces and plays them as a short loop for your coordinator’s camera, a scheme called BC-UR. Or carry the file on a card instead. No cable ever carries your data.

SILENT PAYMENTS

One address you can reuse

One sp1 address you can hand out again and again, without the privacy cost of reusing a normal one. An optional scan key lets your coordinator find those payments but never spend them.

VERIFIED ON DEVICE

Nothing is signed unseen

Every amount, the fee and your change are worked out here rather than taken on trust, and anything risky turns amber before you can sign.

21 LANGUAGES

Including CJK and Cyrillic

Each with its own font. Buttons resize rather than truncate, and the build fails if a key action gets too small to read.

AUDIT IT YOURSELF

The signer proves parts of itself

Your keys are only as good as the randomness behind them. So the device audits its own: 5000 numbers from the chip into a live bar chart, scored for evenness. A lopsided chart means bad randomness. Nothing leaves the device.

03 / the device

The device, and the one mod it needs

One board, no soldering: the Guition JC4880P443C. It arrives with everything KISS needs — a colour touchscreen, a camera for reading QR codes, and a slot for a microSD card. The one thing you have to do yourself is turn the camera around, and that is the rest of this page.

About the radio, said plainly

The chip that runs KISS has no WiFi and no Bluetooth. It cannot go online even if it wanted to. The board does carry a second chip that can, because the board is sold for other products too. The very first thing KISS does on power-up is pin that chip down, before anything else runs. There is no wireless code inside KISS at all.

You do not have to take our word for it. The home screen and Settings both show radio: held in reset, read from the chip each time rather than printed from memory. With a multimeter you can measure the radio chip’s off switch (pin GPIO54) yourself and watch it sit at zero volts.

Flip the camera (one-time mod, required for QR scanning)

The device ships with the camera facing the user, selfie style. Reading a QR code from your coordinator needs it facing the other way. The job is simple but fiddly, so take it slowly:

  1. Lift the screen off with a small suction cup tool to reach the camera behind it.
  2. The camera module is held down by a piece of tape. Pry the tape off gently (fingernails work), flip the camera so the lens faces out the back, and tape it onto the pad that sits behind it.
  3. The three side buttons fall out of the case during this and are hard to seat again. Hold them in place with a tiny dab of glue or tape before closing up.
Take it slow with the suction cup: you are lifting a display, not a case lid.

04 / trust, then flash

Check the download before you trust it

Everything for a release sits on the GitHub Releases tab:

  • kiss-signer-<version>.bin — the firmware
  • SHA256SUMS — its hashes
  • SHA256SUMS.asc — the signature over those hashes
  • kiss_signer_pgp.asc — the key that signed them

Beside them sits kiss-signer-<version>-offline.zip, that same firmware with an install page and this guide, for flashing with no network at all. It is signed on its own by kiss-signer-<version>-offline.zip.asc. 

gpg --import kiss_signer_pgp.asc
gpg --verify SHA256SUMS.asc SHA256SUMS

shasum -a 256 --ignore-missing -c SHA256SUMS     # macOS
sha256sum --ignore-missing -c SHA256SUMS         # Linux

One line of that output matters. The fingerprint must read exactly:

166A CBF3 7786 FCEA A694  96DE 886F 1BFE B84E F1C0

One line looks alarming and is not: WARNING: This key is not certified appears for every key you have not personally signed. The name reads KISS Signer releases, but a name on a key is free text either way. The fingerprint above is the identity.

A fingerprint is only as good as where you read it. Cross-check this one somewhere other than this page.

Windows

Hash with Get-FileHash firmware\kiss-signer-<version>.bin in PowerShell and compare against SHA256SUMS by eye. Verify the signature with Gpg4win.

The install page hashes the firmware in your browser before it offers you the button, and that check runs the same way inside the offline zip. It still does not verify the GPG signature for you, in either place. Do that yourself once per release.

05 / no cable, no computer

Update from an SD card

If your device already runs beta8 or later, this is the easiest route there is. The signer takes its next firmware straight off a card, so there is no cable, no driver, and no computer involved at all.

What to put on the card

From the latest release, take the file ending -update.bin and drop it in the top level of an SD card. That is the whole preparation.

Take the -update.bin file, not the plain .bin. The plain one starts with the bootloader, and a running device looks past that for the application, so it will simply report that there is nothing to install.

Install it from the device

Put the card in, then on the signer go to SETTINGS → FIRMWARE and hold to install.

It checks both signatures against keys built into the device before it writes anything, so a file that is not ours is refused rather than installed. And if the new firmware fails to start, the device puts the old one back by itself.

This route keeps your keys and settings. The browser install erases the whole chip, so use that one for a new device or a very old one, and this one for everything else.

06 / the other way in

Flash it from the command line

The install page needs Chrome, Brave or Edge, because Safari and Firefox will not let a web page talk to a USB device. If you would rather not install a second browser, this does exactly the same job from a terminal.

One tool to install, one command to run. It works on macOS and Linux as written. On Windows use WSL or Git Bash, or retype the last command on one line without the \ marks at the ends. 

pip install esptool        # or: pipx install esptool

# find your device's port:
#   macOS    /dev/cu.usbmodem*
#   Linux    /dev/ttyACM*
#   Windows  COM3, COM4, ...

# check the download first, the same check as the page before this one
shasum -a 256 --ignore-missing -c SHA256SUMS

esptool --chip esp32p4 -p <port> -b 460800 \
  --before default-reset --after no-reset write-flash \
  --flash-mode dio --flash-size 16MB --flash-freq 80m \
  0 kiss-signer-<version>.bin

Then unplug the device, wait three seconds, and plug it back in. It only starts new firmware after a proper power cycle, so a black screen almost always means this step got skipped.

The file you just wrote covers the part of the chip that holds keys, so every flash starts you from clean ground.

07 / nothing reaches the network

Flash on a machine with no internet

The install page fetches the firmware while you flash, so pulling the plug halfway breaks it. This is the same install, packed into one download you carry to a machine that never goes online.

Get the one file

On the latest release, take kiss-signer-<version>-offline.zip, about 6.5 MB. Inside it are the firmware, the install page and this whole guide. Nothing in it reaches out for anything.

Check it before you trust it. The zip is signed on its own, and the command is the same one from checking the download — the folder has a 00-START-HERE.txt with it written out.

Unzip it, then turn the network off

From here nothing needs a connection, so this is the moment to disconnect if you want to.

Start the page

The install page has to be served, not opened as a file. A small program in the folder does that, and shows it to your computer only: double click serve.command on macOS, serve.bat on Windows, or run ./serve.sh on Linux. It prints an address beginning http://localhost. Open that in Chrome, Brave or Edge and flash as normal.

Your computer may warn you about those files. It is right to: they carry no Apple or Microsoft signature, and this project does not buy one. The way that raises no warning at all is to open a terminal in the folder and run python3 serve.py instead.

On Safari or Firefox none of this helps, because the browser itself cannot reach a USB device. Use the command line instead — the firmware is in the same folder, under site/installer/firmware.

08 / read twice, flash once

Flash encryption, the final signer build

On the beta you are using today, someone who steals the device and has the right equipment can read your seed words straight off the chip. The hardened build closes that, by turning two locks on its first boot.

Lock one: the chip scrambles everything it stores

The device makes up an encryption key by itself and writes it where nothing can read it back — not your computer, not us, not you. Everything saved after that is scrambled with it, so someone who pries the memory open reads noise. Your seed words are kept in their own store on the chip, which the scrambling does not cover. That store gets its own lock at the same time.

Lock two: the device stops running strangers’ software

It will only start firmware signed with keys you made and keep, so nobody can quietly swap the signer for a lookalike that phones your seed home.

Both locks turn together, on that first boot. A device that got only the first can never accept the second afterwards, so it is both or neither.

These costs are permanent

  • The first boot changes the chip itself. There is no undo, on any of this.
  • You can never flash it over the cable again. No USB, no install page. That is the whole point — nobody can walk up to your device and quietly rewrite it. Updates still work, but only from an SD card, and only if the update was signed with your own keys. Nothing published for this beta will install on it.
  • That first boot scrambles about 6 MB and can sit on a black screen for several minutes. Do not unplug it until the game menu appears. Cutting the power partway through can kill the device for good.
  • Do this only on a device you intend to keep as your real signer, and run the ordinary release on it first.

The home screen and Settings both show an encryption line, and the device reads it off the chip every time rather than remembering it. Amber OFF means you are not protected yet. When it goes calm, both locks are on — and that is the moment to create keys you actually care about, not before.

09 / hand it to anyone

Getting in the first time

Three rules first. The device will not stop to say them.

  • Every passphrase opens keys. There is no "wrong passphrase" error, and there never will be.
  • The fingerprint tells you which keys you opened. It is on the home screen.
  • This signer is never online. Your coordinator does all the talking to the network.

  1. First time: SET UP THIS SIGNER → NEW SEED WORDS. Pick where the randomness comes from, write the seed words on paper, pass the quiz, choose where they are kept, set your passphrase.
  2. Write the passphrase down too, and keep it somewhere other than the seed words. Together they are your keys; in one place they are one secret, and whoever finds it has everything. Apart, neither half spends on its own.
  3. Then take the optional VERIFY FULL BACKUP rehearsal: type every word from paper and the exact passphrase again. Until both recreate the same fingerprint, I UNDERSTAND keeps a red border. You can skip it, and it is the strongest check you can make before funding.
  4. Later, SETTINGS → BACKUP → SEED WORDS shows them behind a warning, or checks your paper copy without revealing the stored set. A wrong or missing word is reported by position, and neither check alters anything.
  5. Switch to TESTNET while you learn (SETTINGS → SIGNER → NETWORK): free coins, the same screens, nothing at stake. Then walk the checklist below.

Your first TESTNET round trip

This is a visual checklist, not an extra wizard. Complete it once with valueless TESTNET coins before using keys you care about.

KISS re-derives the whole transaction on its own screen and speaks up, in plain words, before you sign, always a soft CAUTION you acknowledge, never a silent surprise:

  • High fee. The fee turns amber if it is a big share of what you send (the rule Krux uses) or an outsized sat/vB rate.
  • Dust and privacy. Spending a tiny coin, or leaving tiny change, is flagged. Both can be used to link and track your addresses. Change below the network dust limit is flagged louder.

Address reuse works differently, because it has to. KISS never sees the chain, so it cannot know which of your addresses were actually paid. Rather than guess, RECEIVE hands out a fresh address each time and keeps a standing reminder to use a new one per payment.

Several cautions stack into one summary; a ? opens a WHY FLAGGED card explaining each and the fix (freeze or label the coin in your coordinator). When any caution is present, the sign button is gated behind an I UNDERSTAND tap. To confirm an incoming address is yours, use Receive → VERIFY and scan what your coordinator shows.

Learn as you go: tap the ? beside any unfamiliar term (RBF, fingerprint, coordinator, dust) for a short card. Some carry a small diagram, like WORDS + PASSPHRASE → FINGERPRINT. Tapping the home fingerprint explains what that code is. On the signed-QR screen, EASY SCAN slows the loop and enlarges the dots for stubborn phone cameras. (Tapping the KISS logo on the game menu locks straight back to the game.)

Settings shows exactly what is on the device, bottom-left: KISS <version> (<commit>), plus an honest note while flash encryption is off.

10 / three storage modes

Where your recovery words are kept

Setup asks this once, and Settings can change it later. All three modes are offered on every build. What differs between them is what an attacker gets from the thing they physically took.

In every mode your passphrase is never stored. It is typed fresh each unlock, and it is what guards the keys that hold your coins. Recovery words on their own derive the base keys, not your passphrase keys.

FLASH

Your seed words stay on the device and it unlocks on its own. On the normal beta they are written in the clear, so someone with the device and the right equipment can read them out of the chip. On a flash-encrypted build the same mode stores them encrypted. The device tells you which of the two you are on, and it works that out by asking the chip directly rather than by trusting what the firmware thinks.

SD CARD

Your seed words are kept on the card as a sealed file that only this device can open, so the card on its own is inert. Insert it before unlocking. The split is the point: a lost card gives up nothing, and the device on its own holds no copy of the card's contents.

The gap this leaves is someone holding both the device and the card. Closing it is what flash encryption adds, by protecting the device key at rest. Until then, SD is still the stronger of the two persistent modes, because FLASH on an unencrypted build stores the seed words in the clear.

AMNESIC

Nothing is written anywhere. The seed words are held in RAM for the session and are gone on lock or power off. You load them again every time, by typing them or by opening an encrypted backup. A device taken while locked holds nothing to find. This is the mode to use for anything that matters on beta firmware.

Changing mode is deliberate and destructive at the old location. KISS writes the new copy, verifies it, and only then removes the old one. Moving a loaded amnesic session to a persistent mode works while it is still unlocked; lock it first and there is nothing left in RAM to migrate, so KISS asks you to load your keys again rather than claim it moved seed words it no longer has.

11 / no hardware required

Try it without hardware

Open the simulator — the signer's own firmware compiled to WebAssembly. Every screen, the real word list, the real derivation, a real signature. Nothing is installed and nothing leaves the tab. One button opens a signer that already has keys, so every screen behind it works immediately.

One thing there is not real: there is no camera behind CAMERA AND TAPS, so that entropy source is filled in for you. The dice and the taps are yours. A browser tab is not a signer — make the keys that matter on the device, offline.
